Reba.

Vision screening for community health workers in Rwanda

STP'26 Hackathon · Ejo Labs · github.com/davidmugisho/reba

A five-minute eye check that runs on a cheap Android phone, offline, and tells a community health worker which patients need a clinic. Reba does not diagnose. It triages, and it says plainly what it does not know.

The problem

Two things take a child's sight in Rwanda, and both are treatable if they are found in time.

Retinoblastoma is a cancer of the eye. It appears in the first years of life. Found early it is largely curable. Missed, it kills the child.

Amblyopia is an eye that never learns to see. One eye is weaker, the brain quietly stops using it, and by about age seven the loss is permanent. The child never complains, because they have never known anything else.

Neither announces itself. Both are found by looking, and the nearest ophthalmologist is hours away. The person who sees the child first is a community health worker visiting the family, and they have nothing to look with. Between a mother notices something in a photograph and a specialist finally examines the child, there is no step at all. So the window closes quietly.

Who it is for

Rwanda's community health workers. They live in the villages they serve, they already visit families, and they are trusted. They carry mid-range Android phones and work where there is often no network. What they lack is an instrument.

What a screening actually looks like

A health worker is visiting a family. The mother mentions that her three-year-old sits very close to the television. The worker opens Reba. There is no network in the village, and none is needed.

1 · Consent. A short script appears, to be read aloud. It says what the check looks for, that it is not a diagnosis, that two photographs will be taken and will stay on this phone, and that the family may stop at any point. The mother agrees out loud.

2 · The vision test. The worker stands a national ID card against the screen and adjusts an outline until the two match. Every ID card in the world is the same size, 85.6 by 53.98 mm, so this tells the app how large a millimetre is on this screen, and the letters that follow are a true physical size rather than an approximation.

The child stands three metres away with one eye covered. A large letter E appears, turned a random way. The child points which way its legs face; the worker taps the matching arrow. Three letters per line, and each line the child reads correctly the next one is smaller. Two mistakes on a line ends that eye. What comes out is a measurement, 6/12 or 6/18, not an impression.

3 · The photographs. In the darkest corner of the room, flash on, one photograph of each eye at arm's length.

4 · The result. One of three bands, large enough to read outdoors: refer, recheck, or no signs today. Behind it, a script to read to the family, and a referral slip to hand over or copy onto the paper form. Five minutes from start to finish.

Why the result can be trusted

Everyone has seen the red-eye effect in family photographs. That red is the flash reflecting off the retina at the back of the eye, which is full of blood vessels. It is a sign that the path from the front of the eye to the back is clear.

When something sits in that path, such as a cataract, a detached retina or a tumour, the light bounces off it instead, and the pupil comes back white. Doctors call it leukocoria, and it is the sign that most often brings a retinoblastoma to attention. Usually it is a parent who spots it in a photograph, by chance, too late.

Reba measures it deliberately. It averages the colour inside each pupil and compares the two eyes. That comparison is the strong part: both photographs come from the same phone, the same flash, seconds apart, so skin tone and lighting cancel out. The patient is their own control.

We tested it against clinical photographs of two different children with retinoblastoma, taken on two different cameras and published in the public domain. Their healthy eyes measured 0.475 and 0.476. Their affected eyes measured 0.381 and 0.375. Both were flagged, neither healthy eye was. The check is in our repository and anyone can run it.

Three rules keep it honest, and they are enforced in the code rather than promised:

  • It never invents. An eye that was not measured is stored as nothing, never as a number. A photograph too dark to read is recorded as unread.
  • The photograph may only raise the level of concern, never lower it. Its thresholds have met few real eyes so far, so it is allowed to add a referral and never to withdraw one. A false alarm costs one consultation; a missed case can cost an eye.
  • It never clears anyone. The mildest thing Reba can say is no signs today, and it says in the same breath that this is not the same as healthy eyes.

How AI and STP'26 tools were applied

WhatHow
Computer vision, on the device Flash light reaching the retina returns red. White means it bounced off something in front of it: a cataract, a detachment, a retinoblastoma. Reba measures that reflex in each photograph and compares the two eyes. No network, no model file, no cost per patient.
EjoChat API After a screening the worker must explain the result to a worried parent. They type the family's question and get an answer in Kinyarwanda, grounded in the screening. It is constrained not to diagnose, not to predict anyone's sight, and to send every clinical question back to the clinician. The written script still works with no signal.
GitHub Full history, with the reasoning for each clinical decision recorded in the code.

What we did not do. There is no trained model yet, and the app says so on the result screen rather than implying one looked. A model needs thousands of eye photographs labelled by ophthalmologists, taken on phones in villages, and no such dataset exists for this setting, because nobody had the tool to collect it. Reba is that tool. Every screening already captures a pair.

Value and impact

  • It works where the need is. Offline, on hardware health workers already own. No connectivity, no per-patient cost, no equipment to buy.
  • Nothing leaves the phone by itself. No account, no server, no sync, no analytics. There is no network code in the app at all. A worker can deliberately export a spreadsheet for the health centre; the photographs are never in it. That is what lets a family agree to a check on their child without agreeing to anything leaving the room.
  • It speaks to the people using it. Kinyarwanda, English, French and German, chosen once at the start of a shift. The consent script and the explanation for the family are read aloud in the language they actually speak.
  • It builds the dataset it needs. Each screening collects a labelled-ready pair of eye photographs in real field conditions. The asset compounds with use.

Next: a clinical partnership to annotate the images, a validated model measured on the same bench, and Kinyarwanda reviewed by a native speaker before any real patient.

Reba means look in Kinyarwanda. Reba does not diagnose. It flags the eyes a nurse or doctor should examine. The decision is always theirs.
