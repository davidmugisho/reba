Reba.

Vision screening for community health workers in Rwanda

STP'26 Hackathon · Ejo Labs · github.com/davidmugisho/reba

A five-minute eye check that runs on a cheap Android phone, offline, and tells a community health worker which patients need a clinic. Reba does not diagnose. It triages, and it says plainly what it does not know.

The problem

Most blindness in Rwanda is avoidable — if someone catches it early. Retinoblastoma kills a child when it is missed and is largely curable when it is caught. Amblyopia becomes permanent by about age seven. The window is narrow and it closes quietly.

But the nearest ophthalmologist is hours away, and the person who sees the child first — a community health worker — has no way to tell a lazy eye from a tired one. There is no tool between "the family noticed something" and "a specialist finally looked". So nobody catches it.

Who it is for

Rwanda's community health workers. They live in the villages they serve, they already visit families, and they are trusted. They carry mid-range Android phones and work where there is often no network. What they lack is an instrument.

The solution

A guided six-step check, about five minutes, entirely on the phone:

  • Visual acuity — a tumbling E at three metres, one eye at a time. The screen is calibrated against a national ID card, so the letters are the correct physical size on any phone. Every adult Rwandan carries that ruler already.
  • Two flash photographs, one per eye.
  • A result readable at arm's length outdoors: refer, recheck, or no signs today — with a script to read aloud to the family in their own language.

Interface in Kinyarwanda, English, French and German. Nothing leaves the phone by itself — no account, no server, no sync, no analytics. A health worker can export a spreadsheet deliberately; photographs are never in it.

How AI and STP'26 tools were applied

Computer vision, on the device Flash light reaching the retina returns red. White means it bounced off something in front of it — a cataract, a detachment, a retinoblastoma. Reba measures that reflex in each photograph and compares the two eyes. No network, no model file, no cost per patient.
EjoChat API After a screening the worker must explain the result to a worried parent. They type the family's question and get an answer in Kinyarwanda, grounded in the screening. What we did not do. There is no trained model yet, and the app says so on the result screen rather than implying one looked. A model needs thousands of eye photographs labelled by ophthalmologists, taken on phones in villages — images nobody has, because nobody had the tool to collect them. Reba is that tool. Every screening already captures them.

Value and impact

  • It works where the need is. Offline, on hardware health workers already own. No connectivity, no per-patient cost, no equipment to buy.
  • It refuses to invent. An eye that was not measured is stored as nothing, never as a number. Photographs too dark to read are recorded as unread. The photo check may add a referral and may never withdraw one — a false positive costs one consultation, a false negative can cost an eye.
  • It builds the dataset it needs. Each screening collects a labelled-ready pair of eye photographs in real field conditions. The asset compounds with use.

Next: a clinical partnership to annotate the images, a validated model measured on the same bench, and Kinyarwanda reviewed by a native speaker before any real patient.

Reba means look in Kinyarwanda. Reba does not diagnose — it flags the eyes a nurse or doctor should examine. The decision is always theirs.
