# Reba - closing ceremony, 3 minutes + 2 minutes Q&A

One speaker. About 430 words, which is three minutes at a calm pace.
Speak slower than feels natural. The room is large and English is a second
language for most of it.

---

## SLIDE 1 - Title (15 sec)

> Good evening. My name is David Mugisho, and this is Reba.
> Reba is Kinyarwanda for "look."
> It is a five minute eye check for community health workers.
> It runs on a cheap phone, and it works offline.

---

## SLIDE 2 - The problem (35 sec)

> Two things take a child's sight in Rwanda. Both can be treated, if they are
> found in time.
>
> Retinoblastoma is a cancer of the eye. Found early, the child lives.
> Missed, the child dies.
>
> Amblyopia is an eye that never learns to see. By the age of seven, the loss
> is permanent.
>
> **Neither one hurts. The child never complains**, because they have never
> known anything else.
>
> The person who sees that child first is a community health worker.
> Today, they have nothing to look with.

*Pause one second after "nothing to look with." Then change slide.*

---

## SLIDE 3 - One screening (45 sec)

> This is what Reba does about that.
>
> The health worker reads a consent script aloud, in the family's language.
>
> Then the vision test. They stand an ID card against the screen and match an
> outline to it. Every card in the world is the same size, so the phone learns
> how big a millimetre is on its own screen. The letters after that are a true
> physical size, not a guess.
>
> The child stands three metres away and points which way the letter faces.
>
> Then one photograph of each eye, with the flash.
>
> Then a result: refer, recheck, or no signs today. And a referral slip for
> the family.
>
> Five minutes. No network at any point.

*If you are running long, this is the slide to trim. Cut the card sentences
and just say "calibrated against an ID card so the letters are a true size."*

---

## SLIDE 4 - The proof (40 sec)

> Why can you trust that result?
>
> Everyone here has seen red eye in a family photograph. That red is the flash
> coming back off the retina. It means the path through the eye is clear.
>
> When something blocks that path, a cataract or a tumour, the pupil comes
> back white.
>
> Reba measures that, and compares the two eyes.
>
> We tested it on clinical photographs of two children with retinoblastoma.
> The healthy eyes measured zero point four seven.
> The affected eyes measured zero point three eight.
>
> **Both were flagged. Neither healthy eye was.**
>
> Same phone, same flash, seconds apart. The patient is their own control.

*Say the numbers slowly. This is the only measured claim in the talk.*

---

## SLIDE 5 - Honesty (25 sec)

> I want to be straight with you about what is not finished.
>
> There is no trained model yet, and the app says so on the result screen.
>
> A model needs thousands of eye photographs, labelled by ophthalmologists,
> taken on phones, in villages. That dataset does not exist for Rwanda.
> Because nobody had the tool to collect it.
>
> **Reba is that tool.** Every screening already captures a pair.

---

## SLIDE 6 - The ask (20 sec)

> So, three things I am asking for.
>
> A clinical partner, to label the photographs the app is already collecting.
>
> A pilot, with health workers in one district.
>
> And a Kinyarwanda speaker, to review the consent script before it meets a
> real patient.
>
> Reba does not diagnose. It tells a nurse which eyes to look at.
> The decision is always theirs.
>
> Thank you.

---

# The 2 minutes of Q&A

Answer short. If you do not know, say you do not know and say what you would
do to find out. That answer scores better than a guess, especially in this room.

### "Is this a medical device? Do you have approval?"

> No, and it is not a diagnostic. It is a triage aid. It tells a health worker
> which children to send to a clinic, the way a thermometer does. Before it
> meets a real patient it needs clinical validation and guidance from Rwanda
> FDA. That is part of what I am asking for tonight.

### "How accurate is it?"

> The vision test is a standard tumbling E, and it is a real measurement.
> The photograph check has been tested against a handful of published clinical
> images, not against a real cohort. So I am not going to quote you a
> sensitivity figure I have not earned. That is exactly what the pilot is for.

### "You are photographing children. What about privacy?"

> Nothing leaves the phone. No account, no server, no sync, no analytics.
> Consent is read aloud and agreed before anything is captured, and the health
> worker can delete a record and its photographs at any time. A worker can
> export a spreadsheet for the health centre, and the photographs are never
> in it.

### "What does it cost to run?"

> Nothing per patient. It runs on phones health workers already carry, and
> there is no server behind it. The real cost is training and the clinical
> partnership.

### "What is the business model?"

> It is not a consumer product and I am not going to charge a family for it.
> The value is the screening infrastructure and the dataset it builds.
> Funding fits health system, NGO, and research partnerships.

### "How is this different from what already exists, like Peek Vision?"

> Peek exists, it is validated, and it is good. I would rather learn from it
> than pretend I am first. What is different here is that Reba is offline
> first, Kinyarwanda first, and it adds the comparison between the two eyes
> in one photograph pair.

### "What if it gets it wrong?"

> It is tuned to be over cautious. It never clears anyone, the mildest thing
> it can say is "no signs today," and the photograph is only allowed to raise
> the level of concern, never to lower it. A false alarm costs one
> consultation. A missed case can cost an eye.

### "Did you build this alone? Did you use AI?"

> I built it, and yes, I used AI assistance for the code. The clinical
> decisions are mine: the calibration method, keeping the photographs off any
> network, Kinyarwanda first, and saying on the result screen that there is no
> model yet. Those are the parts that decide whether this is safe.

*Say yes. This is an AI programme. Everyone used it. Denying it is the only
answer that hurts you.*

### "Can a health worker who does not read use it?"

> The patient never reads anything, they point. The worker taps an arrow.
> The consent script is read aloud, and it is in Kinyarwanda.
